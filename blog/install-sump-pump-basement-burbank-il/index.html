How to Install a Sump Pump in a Basement in Burbank, IL

Installing a sump pump is a project that mixes concrete, plumbing and electrical work. Some homeowners handle a pump swap in an existing pit, and fewer take on a new pit.

This guide walks through the steps so that you can judge how much you want to do yourself.

Decide Between Replacing a Pump and Building a New Pit.

If the basement already has a pit, replacing the pump is a simple job that takes an hour or two. If there is no pit, a new basin must be set into the slab, which takes breaking and removing concrete.

Before you start, find out where the water enters the basement. The pit belongs at the low point where water naturally collects.

The Steps in Order.

This is the sequence for a new pit.

1

Mark and cut.

Mark the pit outline and cut the slab with a saw or a breaker. Wear a respirator, since concrete dust contains silica.

2

Dig and set the basin.

Dig to the depth of the basin, add gravel at the bottom and set the basin level with the floor.

3

Install pump and piping.

Place the pump, connect the check valve and run the discharge pipe through the rim joist or wall.

4

Patch and test.

Pour concrete around the basin, let it cure, then fill the pit with water to confirm the pump cycles.

Worth knowing

Electrical Safety Comes First.

A sump pump runs in a wet place. Use a ground-fault circuit interrupter outlet or breaker, keep the cord away from the pit edge and never use an extension cord. If a dedicated circuit is needed, hire an electrician.

The wet floor around the pit is a shock hazard. Cut power at the panel before reaching for a pump that has stopped working.

Details That Are Easy to Get Wrong.

These are the mistakes behind many repeat calls.

Float placement.

If the float touches the pit wall, the pump will not turn on. Check its swing after you set the pump.

Weep hole.

A small hole in the discharge pipe below the check valve prevents air lock. Many pumps need one.

Discharge location.

The South Stickney Sanitary District states that sump pumps must not be connected to the sanitary sewer. The discharge goes outside, away from the house.

Test the Installation Before You Rely on It.

Fill the pit with water from a hose and watch the pump start and stop. Check that the discharge is flowing and that no water leaks at the joints.

Test again after a rainfall, and mark a date on the calendar to test each spring. A pump that sits idle for months can seize.

Materials and Tools Checklist.

Gather everything before the slab is opened. A pit left open while you run to the store is a safety and mess problem.

  • Pump with a float switch rated for the lift you need.
  • Basin with a lid, and gravel for the bottom.
  • Check valve, unions and PVC pipe with primer and cement.
  • Concrete saw or breaker, and a respirator and eye protection.
  • Concrete mix to patch around the basin.
  • A GFCI outlet and a separate circuit if none exists.

If any item on this list is unfamiliar, the job is a good candidate for a plumber.

How Long the Pit Takes to Cure.

The concrete around the basin needs time to set before you load the pit. Many mixes are firm in a day, but a full cure takes longer. Ask the manufacturer for the schedule of the product you use.

During that period, keep the pump unplugged and keep water out of the pit. Test only after the concrete is firm.

When to Hire Help.

If the pit is wet, the floor is finished or the pump has failed during a storm, call for help. Our sump pump service handles installation and repair at any hour.

Standards Behind the Steps.

Pumps are tested to UL 778, and electrical outlets near water should be GFCI protected under the National Electrical Code, NEC.

Burbank requires sump discharge to go outdoors and not into the sanitary sewer.

What to Do Before You Open the Slab.

Cutting concrete creates dust and noise. A few precautions reduce the risk.

  • Wear a respirator rated for silica dust and eye protection.
  • Wet the cut to reduce dust.
  • Check for pipes and wires below the slab before cutting.
  • Keep the work area clear of other people and pets.
  • Call a plumber if you hit a pipe or water seeps in.

Stop work right away if you find a cable or a gas line.
