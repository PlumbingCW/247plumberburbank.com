Full House Repipe Cost in Burbank, IL: Price Guide

A whole-house repipe is one of the larger plumbing projects. The price depends on the size of the house, the material and how easily the pipes can be reached.

This guide uses national data to show typical ranges and the factors that move the price.

What National Data Says.

Angi reports that the average whole-house repipe costs about $7,500, with most projects between $1,500 and $15,000. It estimates labor at about 70 percent of the total, or about $5,250 on the average project.

These figures are national averages. A quote for a Burbank house depends on its layout, and no website can replace a site visit.

Other Costs in the Project.

A repipe has line items beyond pipe and labor.

Permits.

Angi lists permits at $50 to $500. In Burbank, the Building Department requires a permit for alteration of plumbing.

Drywall repair.

Angi estimates about $300 for small repairs and $900 or more for larger ones. Ask whether patching is included.

Water main replacement.

If the main also needs replacing, Angi lists $600 to $2,500.

Per fixture.

Angi puts the average at about $1,200 per fixture, depending on the amount of piping needed.

Worth knowing

Lead Service Lines: A Bigger Question in Older Homes.

Chicago, which supplies water to the district serving Burbank, says in its 2024 water quality report that homes built before 1986 likely have lead service lines. The South Stickney Sanitary District lists a Lead Service Line Replacement category in its FAQ and a Service Line Certification public notice.

A repipe inside the house does not replace the service line outside. Ask the district about the material of the line, and consider planning both together.

What Raises the Price.

These factors push a repipe higher.

  • Larger or multistory homes.
  • More fixtures and bathrooms.
  • Copper rather than PEX.
  • Walls and ceilings that are hard to open.
  • Moving fixtures or changing the layout.

A plumber can estimate these once they see the house. Our repiping service explains how the work is done.

Repipe the Whole House or a Section.

Not every home needs the whole system replaced.

Section repipe.

  • Replaces a failing run, such as a bathroom group.
  • Lower cost.
  • Leaves old pipe elsewhere.
  • Good when only one area leaks.

Whole-house repipe.

  • Replaces all supply lines.
  • Higher cost.
  • Resolves pressure and rust throughout.
  • Good when several leaks appear.

Spot repairs on a failing system buy time but often move the failure.

Getting Comparable Quotes.

Ask for quotes that list the material, the number of fixtures, patching, permit and the schedule. Confirm that the price is final once the plumber has seen the house, and that no work starts until you approve it.

Our burst pipe repair service handles emergencies while you plan a bigger project.

What Living Through a Repipe Is Like.

Most repipes take a few days, and water is off for part of each day. The crew opens small holes in drywall, runs the new pipe and tests the system before patching. Expect noise, dust and a few hours without water at a time.

Ask how the schedule will work and when the water will be back. A good plan includes a toilet and a sink that stay available as long as possible.

PEX or Copper in a Cold Climate.

Both materials are used in Illinois homes. PEX tolerates freezing better because it can expand slightly, and it needs fewer joints. Copper is rigid and durable, but it can split when it freezes.

Pipes in exterior walls and unheated spaces need insulation with either material. A plumber can suggest routing that keeps lines inside the warm part of the house.

Questions to Ask Before Signing.

These questions bring hidden costs into the open.

  • Does the price include drywall patching and painting?
  • Who pulls the permit and schedules the inspection?
  • What material is the quote based on, and why?
  • How long will the water be off each day?
  • What happens if the crew finds damaged pipes in the wall?

Ask for each answer in writing.

Signs a House May Need Repiping.

Repeated leaks in different parts of the house are the clearest sign. Other signs include low pressure at several fixtures, discolored water that does not clear after running and visible corrosion on exposed pipes.

Houses with older galvanized steel or early plastic piping are more likely to be affected. A plumber can inspect accessible sections and tell you whether a full repipe or a targeted repair makes sense.

Repiping Compared With Repairing.

A single repair costs less today, but a pattern of repairs adds up. If you have fixed three leaks in two years, a repipe may cost less over time than the next several repairs.

Ask the plumber to separate the work into options. One quote can cover the worst section only, and another can cover the whole house, so you can compare the cost of each against the risk of waiting.

Nearby Homes and Older Pipe.

Homes in Stickney and Hickory Hills share similar pipe vintage. Homes with galvanized supply lines are the main repipe candidates.

Ask about service line material at the same time.

What to Do if a Pipe Fails Before the Repipe.

A failing line can turn into a flood. Keep these steps in mind.

  • Find the main shutoff and test that it turns.
  • Shut off the water when you leave for several days.
  • Move valuables away from walls with known leaks.
  • Photograph any stains for your records.
  • Call dispatch if a pipe drips more than a cup a day.

A fast shutoff costs nothing and saves flooring.

Where the Cost Figures Come From.

Cost ranges come from national guides and are not local quotes. PEX follows ASTM F876 and copper follows ASTM B88, and both are accepted under the Illinois Plumbing Code, the state standard found in 77 Ill. Adm. Code 890 and administered by the Illinois Department of Public Health.

The City of Chicago has published information on lead service lines in homes built before 1986, which is a reason to check older piping.
