Recirculating Pump for a Tankless Water Heater: Burbank Guide

The wait for hot water at a far fixture is a common complaint with any heater, and tankless units are no exception. A recirculating pump shortens the wait by moving hot water through the pipes before you open the tap.

This guide explains the options, the trade-offs and what to check before you add one.

Why the Wait Happens.

A tankless heater makes hot water quickly, but the water still has to travel the length of the pipe. The cold water already in the line has to flow out first. The farther the fixture, the longer the wait and the more water goes down the drain.

A recirculating pump keeps hot water moving in a loop, so the pipe near the fixture stays warm.

Recirculation Options Compared.

There are three common ways to set this up.

Recirculation setups
Option.How it works.Trade-offs.
Dedicated return line.A separate pipe returns water from the far fixture to the heater.Best performance, needs a return pipe.
Crossover (comfort) valve.A valve under the far sink lets warm water flow into the cold line.No new pipe, warm cold tap while it runs.
Built-in recirculation.The heater has an internal pump and often a buffer.Needs a supported model.
Timer or demand control.Pump runs on a schedule or button press.Cuts energy use, adds a control.

The right choice depends on the pipe layout and the heater model.

Details That Decide Success.

A pump bolted on without planning wastes energy and can shorten the life of the heater.

Manufacturer approval.

Some tankless models allow external recirculation only with a buffer tank or a specific kit. Check the manual before you buy a pump.

Controls.

A timer or a motion or button trigger keeps the pump from running all day. Constant running wastes gas and wears the unit.

Pipe insulation.

An insulated loop holds heat. Uninsulated pipes in cold basements lose warmth quickly.

Scale and flow.

Hard water scale builds in the heat exchanger faster when the unit fires repeatedly. A flush schedule is more important with recirculation.

Water Savings Fit Burbank's Fixture Rules.

The Illinois Department of Public Health approved Burbank's ordinances adopting the Illinois Plumbing Code in January 2016, and those ordinances require WaterSense-labeled new and replacement fixtures. The rule reflects a local interest in using less water.

A recirculating loop supports the same aim. It trades a small amount of pump energy for the water that would otherwise run down the drain while you wait.

Source: Illinois Department of Public Health approval of Burbank plumbing ordinances, January 13, 2016.

Is a Recirculating Pump Worth It for Your House?

Not every home needs one. These signs suggest a pump is useful.

  • A bathroom or kitchen is far from the heater.
  • You wait a long time for hot water at the same fixtures every day.
  • The home has a return line or room to add one.
  • The heater model supports recirculation.
  • You are willing to set a timer or control.

If your heater is near the fixtures that you use, the wait is short and a pump adds little.

What Installation Involves.

A plumber checks the pipe layout, finds the best route for a return line and mounts the pump near the heater or at the far fixture. For a crossover valve, the valve goes under the farthest sink and connects hot and cold supply lines.

The pump and controls are tested, and the heater is checked for proper temperature and flow after the system runs. See our tankless water heater service page for what installation covers.

Common Problems After Installation.

Complaints come from a few causes. A cold tap that runs warm is usually the crossover valve doing its job. Water that is too hot at the loop is a thermostat setting. A heater that cycles constantly may need a buffer tank or a timer.

If your system cycles on and off often, call for a check. Short cycling shortens the life of the unit.

Comfort and Energy Trade-offs.

Recirculation trades a little energy for convenience. The pump uses small amounts of electricity, and the water in the loop loses heat as it travels, so the heater fires to make it up. A timer that runs the pump only at peak times keeps that cost down.

A demand-activated control, such as a button or a motion sensor, is the most economical choice. It starts the pump when you are about to use the tap and stops when the loop is warm.

Questions to Ask the Installer.

These questions make a recirculation plan concrete.

  • Does the heater model support external recirculation, and with what kit?
  • Will the system use a return line or a crossover valve?
  • How will the pump be controlled and timed?
  • Is the loop insulated, and who handles it?
  • How does the plan affect the heater warranty?

A clear answer to each of these tells you the installer has thought through the system.

Nearby Homes and Long Pipe Runs.

Long pipe runs are common in ranch homes and homes with additions. Homes in Oak Lawn and Stickney frequently have bathrooms at the far end of the house from the basement heater, which is where recirculation helps most.

Dispatch can walk through your layout and suggest the right setup.

Always read the manual for your heater model, because recirculation kits differ by brand.
