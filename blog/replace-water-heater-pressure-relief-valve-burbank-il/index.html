Replace a Water Heater Pressure Relief Valve: Burbank Guide

The temperature and pressure relief valve is the safety device that stops a water heater from becoming a pressure vessel. If it drips, discharges or has never been tested, it needs attention.

This guide covers why valves fail, how a replacement works and when to leave it to a plumber.

What the Relief Valve Is For.

A water heater heats water in a closed tank. If the thermostat fails or pressure climbs, the relief valve opens and lets water out before the tank can rupture. It is the last safety stop.

A relief valve that never opens is not a problem. A valve that opens often is telling you something is wrong with the pressure, the temperature or the valve itself.

Why a Relief Valve Drips.

Dripping is a symptom with several causes. The cause decides the fix.

Relief valve symptoms
What you notice.Likely cause.What to do.
Slow drip, no heater problems.Worn valve seat or mineral buildup.Test the valve. Replace if it keeps dripping.
Drips when the heater is heating.Thermal expansion with no expansion tank.Install an expansion tank.
Steady stream.Excess pressure or overheating.Shut off the heater and call a plumber.

How a Replacement Is Done.

This is the overview a plumber follows. It is not a substitute for the manufacturer's instructions.

Shut the heater down.

Switch off the gas control or the breaker, then close the cold water shutoff above the heater.

Relieve pressure and drain.

Open a hot tap upstairs to release pressure, then drain a few gallons from the tank so that the water level is below the valve.

Remove and match the valve.

Unscrew the old valve and the discharge pipe. The new valve must match the tank's pressure rating and have a temperature rating that fits the unit, often 150 psi and 210 degrees Fahrenheit. Check the tank label.

Install and test.

Thread the new valve in with pipe thread sealant, reconnect the discharge pipe and refill the tank. Lift the test lever to confirm flow, then check for leaks.

Worth knowing

Discharge Pipe Rules That Matter.

The discharge pipe must be the same size as the valve outlet, run downward and end within a few inches of the floor or drain. It must not be capped, threaded at the end or fitted with a valve.

A capped or reduced discharge pipe defeats the valve. This is one of the most common errors we see in old installations.

What a Burbank Inspection Checks on a Gas Water Heater.

The City of Burbank Building Department offers a free inspection on gas water heater replacement. The inspection covers ventilation, the safety valve and the connections, according to the department. A permit is required for construction or alteration of plumbing, and all contractors must be licensed.

If you replace a relief valve as part of a larger job, the inspector looks at the discharge line and the valve rating, so correct work the first time saves a return visit.

Source: City of Burbank Building Department, 6530 W 79th Street, 708-599-5500 option 2.

When to Call a Plumber Instead of Doing It Yourself.

Replacing the valve is within some homeowners' skill, but these situations call for help.

  • The valve is discharging steadily or the water is very hot.
  • The tank is a gas model and you smell gas.
  • The valve will not unscrew, or threads are damaged.
  • There is no expansion tank and the valve drips on every cycle.
  • You are not sure of the valve rating for your tank.

Our water heater repair service tests the valve, checks the pressure and installs the expansion tank when needed.

Pressure Is Often the Real Cause.

A drip every time the heater runs often points to thermal expansion. Water expands when it is heated, and in a closed system it has nowhere to go. An expansion tank gives it room.

Check the home's water pressure with a gauge on an outdoor spigot. Pressure above about 80 psi strains valves and fixtures, and a pressure reducing valve may be the better fix.

Testing the Valve Without Making a Mess.

Place a bucket under the end of the discharge pipe and lift the test lever for a second or two. You should hear a rush of water and see it flow into the bucket. Release the lever and watch that the valve seats and the flow stops.

If the valve keeps dripping after a test, mineral particles may be holding the seat open. A valve that drips after testing is due for replacement, and one that does not flow at all must be replaced right away.

Common in Nearby Towns Too.

We see the same pattern in older homes in Bridgeview and Stickney, where heaters have been in place for many years and discharge pipes have been modified. A short inspection can find these problems before they become failures.

Standards for Relief Valves.

Relief valves for hot water systems are made to ANSI Z21.22. The discharge pipe must end where escaping water or steam cannot hurt anyone.

These rules appear in the Illinois Plumbing Code, the state standard found in 77 Ill. Adm. Code 890 and administered by the Illinois Department of Public Health.
