Tankless Water Heater Guide for Burbank, IL Homeowners

An instant or tankless water heater heats water as it flows, so there is no tank to fill. That is a real convenience, but it moves the work to the gas line, the vent and the maintenance schedule.

This guide explains the sizing questions that decide whether a tankless unit fits a Burbank home.

How a Tankless Heater Works.

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor tells the unit to light a burner or energize elements. Cold water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves hot. When the tap closes, the unit shuts down.

The unit has a flow limit. A single shower is easy, and several fixtures at once can exceed what the unit can heat to your set temperature.

Typical Fixture Flow Rates.

Federal rules limit showerheads to 2.5 gallons per minute, and WaterSense-labeled showerheads use 2.0 gallons per minute or less. Other fixtures vary. This table shows common demand figures to help you add them up.

Winter Is the Hard Test.

A tankless heater must raise the incoming water temperature to your setting. Winter supply water in the Chicago area can fall into the 40s Fahrenheit or lower, so a unit that handles two showers in July may struggle with the same load in January.

Ask for a sizing based on winter temperature rise, not summer. A unit sized for the cold months will cope year-round.

The Gas Line and the Vent.

Most Burbank homes use a gas tank heater today, and a tankless unit changes two parts of the installation.

Gas line capacity.

Tankless units draw a high volume of gas for a short time. The existing line and meter may need upgrading to deliver it.

Venting.

Condensing models use plastic pipe and a condensate drain. Non-condensing units need stainless steel venting. Both must follow manufacturer rules and local code.

Electrical and water.

The unit needs an outlet for controls, and isolation valves for flushing. Hard water makes these valves vital.

Chicago Water Is Moderately Hard.

Chicago supplies Lake Michigan water to the South Stickney Sanitary District, and its 2013 quarterly lab reports listed hardness of 134 to 143 mg/L as calcium carbonate, about 8 grains per gallon by our conversion. That is a 2013 figure, and the district can share the current value.

Hard water forms scale on the heat exchanger of a tankless unit, which cuts flow and raises the risk of error codes. A yearly flush through isolation valves is part of owning one in this area.

Source: City of Chicago Department of Water Management quarterly water quality reports (2013). Conversion to grains per gallon is ours.

Questions to Ask Before You Buy.

These questions prevent most regrets.

  • What is the unit's flow at a winter temperature rise?
  • Does the existing gas line support the unit's peak demand?
  • Where will the vent terminate, and is it code compliant?
  • Are isolation valves included for flushing?
  • Who handles the permit and the city inspection?

Permit and Code Basics in Burbank.

The City of Burbank Building Department says a permit is required for construction or alteration of plumbing and that all contractors must be licensed. It also offers a free inspection on gas water heater replacement that looks at ventilation, the safety valve and the connections.

Ask the installer to confirm the permit steps before work starts. The department is at 6530 W 79th Street and answers at 708-599-5500, option 2.

A Simple Yearly Routine for Tankless Owners.

A tankless unit asks for little attention, but the attention it needs is regular. This routine keeps the heat exchanger clear and the unit safe.

  • Flush the heat exchanger once a year with a descaling solution or white vinegar through the service valves.
  • Clean the inlet water filter screen when you flush.
  • Check the vent and the condensate drain for blockage.
  • Look at the error code history if the unit stores one.
  • Test the carbon monoxide alarm near the unit.

A unit that is flushed on schedule keeps its rated flow. A unit that is ignored slowly loses it, and the first sign is often a shower that never gets quite hot enough.

Budgeting Without a Number You Cannot Trust.

Total cost for a tankless job is the unit plus the gas line, the vent, the plumbing changes and the permit. Two homes with the same unit can end up with very different totals because of those extras.

The honest way to budget is to ask for a written price after a site visit. Compare that price with a tank replacement, and decide whether the added cost is worth hot water on demand and the extra wall space.

Where Tankless Fits Best.

Tankless works well for smaller households, for homes with limited floor space and for people who want hot water on demand. It also suits additions where running a tank would be difficult.

Homes in nearby Chicago Ridge and Hickory Hills often have basements that can accommodate either style, so the choice comes down to usage and budget.
